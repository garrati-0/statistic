#10 Simulating the Poisson Process (The "Law of Small Numbers")

This homework simulates a counting process. We are given a time interval $T=1$ and an average event rate $\lambda$. The events are "independent and uniform in time." This is the definition of a Poisson Process.

We are asked to *approximate* this continuous-time process using a discrete-time model. We do this by dividing the interval $T=1$ into $n$ tiny subintervals. The probability of an event in any one of these subintervals is set to $p = \lambda / n$.

1. The Simulation: A Binomial Process

What we have constructed is a sequence of $n$ independent Bernoulli trials (each subinterval is a trial: "event occurs" or "event does not occur"). The total number of events $K$ in the interval $T=1$ is the sum of these $n$ trials.

This means our simulation *directly* models the Binomial Distribution: $$ K \sim B(n, p) \quad \text{where } p = \lambda / n $$

2. The Target: The Poisson Process

This simulation is a classic demonstration of the "Law of Small Numbers" (or Poisson limit theorem). This theorem states that as $n \to \infty$ and $p \to 0$ in such a way that the mean $\lambda = np$ remains constant, the Binomial distribution $B(n, p)$ converges to the Poisson distribution with parameter $\lambda$. $$ \lim_{n \to \infty} B(n, \lambda/n) = \text{Poisson}(\lambda) $$

The resulting distribution for the number of events $K$ in the interval is given by the Poisson probability mass function (PMF): $$ P(K=k) = \frac{e^{-\lambda} \lambda^k}{k!} $$

3. Interpretation of $\lambda$ and Properties

  • Interpretation of $\lambda$: The parameter $\lambda$ is the rate parameter. Since $T=1$, $\lambda$ represents the *average* or *expected* number of events in the interval. If we ran the simulation many times, the average of all the final event counts would be very close to $\lambda$. That is, $E[K] = \lambda$.
  • Theoretical Properties: The Poisson process has two key properties:
    1. Independent Increments: The number of events in one time interval is independent of the number of events in any *non-overlapping* interval.
    2. Memorylessness: The probability of an event occurring in the next instant does not depend on how long we have been waiting. (This implies the time *between* events, or inter-arrival time, follows an Exponential distribution).
