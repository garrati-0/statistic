#4 Demonstrating the Law of Large Numbers

In probability theory, the Law of Large Numbers (LLN) is a fundamental theorem. It states that as the number of trials of an experiment increases, the average of the results obtained should get closer and closer to the *true* theoretical probability.

The Experiment: A Virtual Coin Toss

For this assignment, our experiment is a simple coin toss. We'll use JavaScript's Math.random() to generate a number between 0 and 1.

  • If the number is < 0.5, we'll call it "Tails" (and assign it a value of 0).
  • If the number is >= 0.5, we'll call it "Heads" (and assign it a value of 1).

The theoretical probability of getting "Heads" is exactly 0.5 (or 50%). If we only toss the coin 10 times, we might get 7 heads (a frequency of 0.7) or 3 heads (a frequency of 0.3) just by chance. The LLN states that if we toss the coin 10,000 times, our observed frequency will be extremely close to 0.5.

Visualizing the Law

This simulation will track two metrics across many tosses:

  1. The Random Walk (Path): As requested, this line (in blue) shows the cumulative sum of heads. It goes up by 1 when we get a "1" (Heads) and stays horizontal when we get a "0" (Tails). This line represents the total *count* of heads.
  2. The Running Frequency (Average): This line (in orange) is the one that demonstrates the law. It is calculated at each step as (Total Heads so far) / (Total Tosses so far).

Watch how the orange line starts erratically (it can be 1.0 or 0.0 after the first toss!) but quickly stabilizes and "hugs" the 0.5 target line as the number of tosses increases.

Why Does This Happen?

The Law of Large Numbers works because short-term deviations from the average are rendered insignificant by the sheer volume of "normal" events over the long run.

Imagine you toss the coin 100 times and, by pure chance, you get 60 heads (a 0.6 frequency). You are "up" by 10 heads from the expected 50. To get back to 0.5, you would need to get 10 *fewer* heads than average in the *next* 100 tosses (i.e., 40 heads). This is unlikely.

What's more likely is that over the *next* 9,900 tosses, you will get something very close to the average, like 4,950 heads.

  • Total Heads: 60 (from first 100) + 4950 (from next 9900) = 5010
  • Total Tosses: 100 + 9900 = 10,000
  • New Frequency: 5010 / 10,000 = 0.501

Even though you were never "corrected" for that initial lucky streak (you are still "up" by 10 from the 5000 you'd expect), that 10-head deviation becomes a tiny drop in the ocean of 10,000 trials. The *frequency* aligns with the probability, even if the *raw count* doesn't perfectly match the expectation.
